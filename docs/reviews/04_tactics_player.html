Let me set expectations honestly

When someone says "tactics," my brain loads a grid: cover, flanking, zones of control, line of sight, the agony of one tile. Isekai Strategem is not that. It's a squad battler — two lines of three trading turns, closer to Slay the Spire's combat or a party-based JRPG than to XCOM. That's a legitimate genre and it can be excellent. But measured against the tactics games I actually play, it's missing two of the pillars I come for, and it has one variance problem that undercuts the rest.

The meta-layer is genuinely strong

Where this game earns its "strategem" is before the battle and between the lines:

  • Loadout theorycraft. 60 triggers + 9 Black Triggers + perks + a Trion-capacity budget is a real build sandbox. I can spend an hour optimizing a squad. That's my favorite kind of homework.
  • Team Efficiency Grade as a risk/reward dial. A higher Grade gives dice advantages but less post-battle XP; a scrappy low Grade earns more. That inverse curve is a clever anti-snowball, straight out of the "rubber-band without cheating" playbook. I respect it a lot.
  • Queue + 6-phase resolution. You commit a turn's actions and they resolve in a fixed phase order (buffs → control → attacks, with a Team-Spirit tiebreak). That's a planning puzzle most battlers lack — a little bit of Into the Breach's "order of operations matters."
  • Counters and combos give me a reaction economy and setup→payoff lines to build around. Excellent.
KeepThe pre-battle build sandbox + TEG risk/reward + queue resolution are your tactical identity. Don't lose them.

Pillar gap #1: no positioning means half the genre is absent

Every decision in a tactics game ultimately routes through space: where I stand determines what I threaten and what threatens me. Here, "melee" vs "ranged" are flavor tags — nobody moves, nobody closes a gap, nobody holds an angle. So the entire family of decisions I love — flank or hold, split or group, bait an overextension — simply doesn't exist. You don't need a full XCOM grid, but you need a spatial axis (front/back rows, 3 lanes, or range bands) so that where an operator sits is a choice with consequences.

Pillar gap #2: initiative is a coin flip — in a lethal game

The variance that hurts mostWho acts first is decided by an even 50/50 coin flip. In a game where a single attack can delete a unit, first-turn is often the whole game — and it's pure luck. Modern tactics treats initiative as the most contested resource: Banner Saga's turn wheel, XCOM concealment/overwatch, Into the Breach's perfect turn order. Handing it to a coin toss is the least tactical decision the game makes.

You already compute a Team Efficiency Grade — that's a natural, earned lever to influence initiative (or at least to weight the flip). Randomness here should be something I can build against, not a raw 50/50.

Pillar gap #3: variance compounds instead of being mitigated

Great tactics games are careful about where they put randomness. Into the Breach removes to-hit RNG entirely and gives perfect information. XCOM keeps hit% but lets you stack overwatch, flanks, and abilities to control it. This game stacks its randomness:

Random layerEffectMitigation available?
Coin-flip initiativeWho acts first (often decisive)None
d20 to-hit (×2, opposed)Whiff chance on planned linesAdvantage exists, but muted
Buildable crit (up to ~75%)Doubles already-large hitsYou can stack it — so can they

None of these is fatal alone; together they mean a well-planned turn can lose to a coin flip + a lucky crit, and I can't fully insure against it. Tactics players will forgive hard fights; they won't forgive feeling that the dice, not the plan, decided it.

Information & AI

Good instincts here: enemy loadouts are hidden (fog of war) and Mind's Eye is a tool to pierce it — that's a real tactical information layer, and the public-vs-hidden intel gating on the new clickable portraits is a nice touch. What I can't see is the enemy's queued actions, so planning against the AI is guesswork rather than the readable telegraph Into the Breach spoiled me on. And single-player tactics lives or dies on AI quality — the roadmap lists AI tuning as future work, so I'll reserve judgment, but a squad-builder with an exploitable AI loses its tension fast.

Build diversity is being eaten by outliers

My theorycraft is only fun if many builds are viable. A few triggers are efficient enough to be auto-includes and collapse the meta: whirlwind_slash (cd 1, 12 Trion, 3-target, +bleed — the best damage-per-Trion in the game), twin_fang_strike (cd 1 single-target spam), and longshot (a ~104-damage one-shot). When "just bring the efficient thing" wins, my build sandbox shrinks to one build. Tighten these to a damage-per-Trion-per-cooldown budget and the whole sandbox opens back up.

My tactics-player bottom line

  1. Own your genre. Either add a spatial pillar (rows/lanes/range bands) to become a real tactics game, or market as a squad-builder battler and double down on the build+queue meta.
  2. Kill the coin-flip initiative. Make first-turn an earned, buildable outcome (TEG-weighted or drafted).
  3. Consolidate randomness. Fewer, more controllable random layers; give me tools to insure a plan (guaranteed-hit floor, bounded crit).
  4. Protect build diversity by budgeting the outlier triggers.
  5. Telegraph more of the enemy's turn so planning beats guessing.
